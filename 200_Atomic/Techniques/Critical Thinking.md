# Critical Thinking

## Core Idea

Analysis of facts followed by reasoned conclusions, while recognizing cognitive biases and logical fallacies that distort clear thinking.

## Why It Matters

Most people accept conclusions without examining the reasoning. Critical thinking protects you from manipulation, improves decision quality, and builds intellectual independence.

## Key Points

- **Critical thinking** = Analyzing facts → Building conclusions
- **Cognitive biases** = Brain functions that prevent clear thinking
- **Manipulation** = Exploiting logical errors for personal gain
- **Argumentation forms** - Structure of logical reasoning
- **Fallacy recognition** - Identifying flawed arguments

### Common Argument Fallacies

1. **Tu quoque** - "And what have YOU achieved?" (attacking credibility instead of argument)
2. **Ad hominem** - Attacking the person rather than their argument
3. **Ad ignorantiam** - "I don't know this, therefore I don't believe you"
4. **Burden of proof** - "Prove UFOs exist. No, YOU prove they don't!" (shifting responsibility)
5. **Ad populum** - "We're the majority, you're wrong" (appeal to popularity)
6. **Non sequitur** - Conclusions that don't follow from premises
7. **Straw man** - Misrepresenting opponent's argument to attack easier version
8. **Poisoning the well** - Making good things seem bad preemptively
9. **Begging the question** - Answering question with another question

## Examples

### Example 1: Technical Decision
**Claim:** "We should use microservices because Google uses them"

**Critical analysis:**
- Fallacy: Ad populum (appeal to authority/popularity)
- Missing: Our context vs. Google's scale
- Better question: What problems do WE need to solve?

### Example 2: Self-Development Content
**Claim:** "Wake up at 5 AM or you'll never be successful"

**Critical analysis:**
- False dichotomy (5 AM OR failure)
- Survivorship bias (successful people who wake at 8 AM are invisible)
- Missing: Individual chronotypes, sleep quality vs. wake time

## How to Apply

**For evaluating arguments:**
1. **Identify the claim** - What's actually being said?
2. **Find the premises** - What evidence supports this?
3. **Check logic** - Do conclusions follow from premises?
4. **Spot fallacies** - Which of the 9 types appear?
5. **Question assumptions** - What's taken for granted?

**For building arguments:**
1. State claim clearly
2. Provide evidence (facts, data, examples)
3. Show logical connection (premises → conclusion)
4. Address counterarguments
5. Avoid fallacies

**Daily practice:**
- Read one persuasive article/video
- Identify 3 claims
- Map the argument structure
- Find 1 fallacy or weak point
